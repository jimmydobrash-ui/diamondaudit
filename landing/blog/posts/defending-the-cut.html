The hardest part of running a tryout has never been watching the players. It's the phone call three days later.

A parent asks why their kid didn't make it. It's a fair question, asked in good faith, and you owe them a real answer. So you go looking for one — and what you find is a stack of clipboards in three different handwritings, a spreadsheet somebody started and abandoned halfway through Saturday, and your own memory of a kid you watched for about eleven minutes two weekends ago.

You know the call was right. You just can't show your work.

Every coach scores differently, and nobody talks about it

Put four coaches on the same field and ask them to rate arm strength out of ten. One of them has been coaching high school for fifteen years and grades against varsity. One of them is a parent volunteer who's thrilled by any throw that arrives on a line. Their sevens are not the same seven. Neither of them is wrong, exactly — they're just calibrated to different things, and no one has ever said out loud what a seven is supposed to mean.

That gap doesn't show up while you're scoring. It shows up at the end, when you stack the numbers together and something looks off, and you can't tell whether a player is genuinely borderline or whether he just happened to rotate through the two toughest graders.

Speed is the other problem

Tryouts move. You've got a field for a fixed number of hours, players cycling through stations, and a clock that doesn't care how thorough you're being. Anything that slows a coach down gets abandoned by the second hour — which is exactly why the detailed evaluation form somebody printed out ends up half-filled and face-down under a bat bag.

Whatever you use has to survive contact with a real tryout: usable one-handed, on a phone, standing on a field, while a kid is already stepping into the box.

What we actually wanted

Not analytics. Not a scouting database. Three specific things:

  • One standard. Everybody's seven means the same thing, because the scale says what it means before anyone starts scoring.
  • One picture. Every coach's evaluation rolls into a single shared view, instead of four private opinions that have to be reconciled by argument at the end of the day.
  • A record. When the call comes, you can open the actual evaluation — what was scored, by whom, alongside every other player in that age group.

That last one matters more than it sounds. The point isn't to win an argument with a parent. It's that a decision you can explain is usually a decision you made more carefully in the first place. Knowing you'll have to show your work changes how you do the work.

It doesn't make the cut easy

Being honest about the limits here: no tool makes telling a thirteen-year-old he didn't make the team feel good. Nothing turns a judgment call into a math problem, and anything claiming otherwise is selling you something.

What it does is narrow the part of the decision that's guesswork, and make the rest of it something you can stand behind — to a parent, to your staff, and to yourself.

That's the whole idea. Built by a coach who got tired of defending cuts with a stack of clipboards.
